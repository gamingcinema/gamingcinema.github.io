Story

God of War (2018) is Santa Monica Studio's PS4-exclusive franchise reboot — a sequel to the original Greek mythology trilogy that completely reinvents its protagonist, tone, and gameplay structure. The original trilogy followed the Spartan warrior Kratos through a rage-driven decade of god-killing across Olympus, climaxing with his destruction of the Greek pantheon and his apparent death in God of War III (2010). The 2018 reboot opens an indeterminate number of years later in the cold forests of Midgard — the central Norse mythological realm — with Kratos, having fled Greece in the aftermath of the original trilogy, living a quiet hermit's life with a Norse wife named Faye and their young son Atreus.

The campaign opens with Faye's death. The opening sequence is the funeral pyre — Kratos and Atreus burn her body in the forest, with the campaign's central premise established in the silence of the act: Faye's final wish was for her ashes to be scattered from the highest peak in all the realms. The campaign is the journey to that peak. Kratos has never told Atreus about his past — the boy does not know his father is a god, does not know what his father did to the Greek pantheon, does not know that his own godhood manifests as the recurring fevered illness that has shaped his childhood. The campaign's central character work is the slow, deliberately uncomfortable opening of that conversation.

The campaign's middle act unfolds across the nine realms — Midgard, Alfheim, Helheim, Jotunheim, Niflheim, Muspelheim — with the franchise's signature combat reinvented around the Leviathan Axe, a Norse-forged ice axe that Kratos throws and recalls in the same fluid traversal animation. Father and son encounter the Norse pantheon's surviving figures: the dwarven smiths Brok and Sindri, the severed-head world-shepherd Mimir, the Vanir witch Freya (who saves Atreus's life during a divinity-induced collapse), and the antagonist who has been pursuing them across the campaign — Baldur, the Aesir prince whose mother's protective spell has made him invulnerable to virtually every form of physical harm.

The campaign's central reveals land in its third act, culminating at Jotunheim — the lost giants' realm Faye sent Kratos and Atreus to find. The reveals arrive in sequence: Baldur is Freya's son, his invulnerability a curse she imposed to protect him from her own prophetic visions of his death, his pursuit of Freya the result of that curse's destruction of his physical sensation; Faye was a Giant, the last surviving member of the Jotunheim people, deliberately married into Kratos's exile to position her son for the Norse prophecy; Atreus's true name is Loki, given to him by Faye in the giant tradition Kratos was not told about. The campaign's climactic confrontation stages Baldur's attempt to kill Freya rather than face the half-life her protective curse has condemned him to. Kratos intervenes, kills Baldur to save Freya from her own son's matricide, and Freya — released from her son's curse but unable to forgive her son's killer — swears vengeance on Kratos. The Jotunheim murals reveal the broader prophecy: Atreus, as Loki, is central to the coming Ragnarok, and the mural depicting Atreus holding a dying man with an eerie energy passing between them positions the second installment's central character question. The campaign closes with the post-credits visit from a cloaked stranger whose hammer identifies him as Thor, the Aesir storm god whose arrival at Kratos's home positions the broader Norse pantheon's reckoning with the man who killed Olympus.

Story

God of War Ragnarok is Santa Monica Studio's 2022 direct sequel to the 2018 franchise reboot, closing the Norse saga with the eponymous mythological apocalypse and its consequences for both Kratos and Atreus. The previous campaign closed with the Aesir prince Baldur dead by Kratos's hand, Freya turned against the family in vengeance for her son, Thor's arrival at the family's home with the broader Aesir pantheon's reckoning to come, and the giant prophecy of Atreus's central role in Ragnarok positioned for the trilogy's continuation. Ragnarok opens roughly three years later, in the depths of the prophesied Fimbulwinter — a centuries-long winter the Norse mythological tradition stages as Ragnarok's harbinger.

The campaign opens in the family's snowed-over hermit's cabin. Kratos and Atreus have spent the winter training and hunting; Atreus, now in his early teens, is increasingly determined to investigate his Loki identity independently of Kratos's protective restrictions. The campaign's opening hour is interrupted by the unannounced arrival of Thor, who appears at the cabin with Odin at his side — the Aesir patriarch attempting a diplomatic offer rather than a punitive raid. Odin's request is straightforward: Kratos and Atreus must stop investigating the prophecy. Kratos refuses. The Aesir depart with the implicit threat that the next visit will not be diplomatic. The campaign's central political conflict is committed.

The campaign's middle act expands the franchise's playable scope substantially. Atreus is now a fully playable character in his own sustained narrative threads, with the campaign cutting between Kratos's investigation of the prophetic Yggdrasil infrastructure and Atreus's parallel infiltration of Asgard under cover of an alliance with Odin himself. The party expands to include Freya (whose vendetta against Kratos is mended through the campaign's most acclaimed character work), the giant warrior Angrboda (Atreus's age-peer and Loki's mythological partner), the boar-spirit Hildisvini, and ultimately the freed Tyr — the Aesir god of war whose disappearance has shaped the campaign's political opening. The campaign's central reveal lands at the midpoint: the Tyr Kratos and Atreus freed from Odin's prison is not Tyr. He is Odin in disguise, infiltrating the protagonists' coalition to gather intelligence on the giants' surviving prophetic infrastructure.

The campaign's third act stages the coordinated convergence of the Ragnarok prophecy. The dwarven smith Brok — one of the franchise's most beloved supporting characters across both installments — is murdered by Odin in the protagonists' workshop, the killing staged with no music in close-up, with Sindri's subsequent grief reframing the campaign's emotional register entirely. The protagonists' alliance with Asgard's dissidents (Thor's daughter Thrud, Tyr the real, the Jotunn refugees) is formalized. The Gjallarhorn sounds and Ragnarok begins, with the campaign's most acclaimed mass-combat sequence staging the assault on Asgard with the franchise's full character roster fighting in coordination.

The climactic sequence stages Kratos's confrontation with Thor and Odin in sequence. Kratos defeats Thor in single combat — the most acclaimed boss encounter of the franchise's modern era, staged across multiple environmental phases — and spares him with the line "Be better." Thor considers it. Odin, hearing his son's hesitation, kills Thor on the spot. The campaign's central thematic argument lands in the silence following: that the Aesir father has been the consistent toxic force across the campaign's full cast, that Kratos's hard-won transformation from rage to deliberate ethics is the only alternative the franchise has offered to that toxicity, and that breaking generational violence requires the cost of sustained moral commitment rather than the satisfaction of vengeance. Kratos defeats Odin in the campaign's final boss encounter, and Atreus seals Odin's soul into a tiny ball of marble-sized magic that Sindri immediately shatters in his grief. The campaign's epilogue stages the surviving coalition's rebuilding work — Freya as the restored Valkyrie Queen, the freed Tyr resuming his diplomatic role, Kratos accepting the role of the realms' protector that the Jotunheim murals had already prophesied for him — and Atreus's quiet departure to pursue his Loki identity on his own terms. The campaign closes with Kratos's mural prophecy fulfilled: the man who killed Olympus has chosen, finally, to become the god worth following.
