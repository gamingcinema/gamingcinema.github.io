Story

God of War (2018) is Santa Monica Studio's PS4-exclusive franchise reboot — a sequel to the original Greek mythology trilogy that completely reinvents its protagonist, tone, and gameplay structure. The original trilogy followed the Spartan warrior Kratos through a rage-driven decade of god-killing across Olympus, climaxing with his destruction of the Greek pantheon and his apparent death in God of War III (2010). The 2018 reboot opens an indeterminate number of years later in the cold forests of Midgard — the central Norse mythological realm — with Kratos, having fled Greece in the aftermath of the original trilogy, living a quiet hermit's life with a Norse wife named Faye and their young son Atreus.

The campaign opens with Faye's death. The opening sequence is the funeral pyre — Kratos and Atreus burn her body in the forest, with the campaign's central premise established in the silence of the act: Faye's final wish was for her ashes to be scattered from the highest peak in all the realms. The campaign is the journey to that peak. Kratos has never told Atreus about his past — the boy does not know his father is a god, does not know what his father did to the Greek pantheon, does not know that his own godhood manifests as the recurring fevered illness that has shaped his childhood. The campaign's central character work is the slow, deliberately uncomfortable opening of that conversation.

The campaign's middle act unfolds across the nine realms — Midgard, Alfheim, Helheim, Jotunheim, Niflheim, Muspelheim — with the franchise's signature combat reinvented around the Leviathan Axe, a Norse-forged ice axe that Kratos throws and recalls in the same fluid traversal animation. Father and son encounter the Norse pantheon's surviving figures: the dwarven smiths Brok and Sindri, the severed-head world-shepherd Mimir, the Vanir witch Freya (who saves Atreus's life during a divinity-induced collapse), and the antagonist who has been pursuing them across the campaign — Baldur, the Aesir prince whose mother's protective spell has made him invulnerable to virtually every form of physical harm.

The campaign's central reveals land in its third act, culminating at Jotunheim — the lost giants' realm Faye sent Kratos and Atreus to find. The reveals arrive in sequence: Baldur is Freya's son, his invulnerability a curse she imposed to protect him from her own prophetic visions of his death, his pursuit of Freya the result of that curse's destruction of his physical sensation; Faye was a Giant, the last surviving member of the Jotunheim people, deliberately married into Kratos's exile to position her son for the Norse prophecy; Atreus's true name is Loki, given to him by Faye in the giant tradition Kratos was not told about. The campaign's climactic confrontation stages Baldur's attempt to kill Freya rather than face the half-life her protective curse has condemned him to. Kratos intervenes, kills Baldur to save Freya from her own son's matricide, and Freya — released from her son's curse but unable to forgive her son's killer — swears vengeance on Kratos. The Jotunheim murals reveal the broader prophecy: Atreus, as Loki, is central to the coming Ragnarok, and the mural depicting Atreus holding a dying man with an eerie energy passing between them positions the second installment's central character question. The campaign closes with the post-credits visit from a cloaked stranger whose hammer identifies him as Thor, the Aesir storm god whose arrival at Kratos's home positions the broader Norse pantheon's reckoning with the man who killed Olympus.
